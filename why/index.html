PhyloGuessr

Why this stuff matters

Phylogenetics — the study of evolutionary relationships — reveals that all life shares a common ancestor and maps how closely every organism is related. Many genes involved in human disease have counterparts in distant relatives like flies and yeast; because they're cheap and fast to work with, scientists use them to study disease and test treatments.

Medicine and disease

  • Drug discovery — phylogeny guides which organisms to test in: mice share many disease genes with us, so nearly every approved drug was tested in rodents first. Zebrafish, C. elegans, and fruit flies are used for early screening because they breed fast and share key disease pathways with humans
  • Cancer — two-thirds of genes implicated in human cancers have counterparts in fruit flies. Nobel Prize-winning research on the cell cycle was done in yeast, and programmed cell death was discovered in C. elegans worms
  • Metagenomics — phylogenetics classifies the thousands of microbial species in a gut microbiome sample
  • Comparative genomics — comparing human and mouse genomes reveals regulatory DNA sequences that would otherwise be nearly impossible to identify

Genetic tools

Many of our most powerful genetic tools were adapted from mechanisms that evolved in other organisms.

  • CRISPR — a bacterial immune system repurposed to precisely edit human genes. The FDA approved the first CRISPR therapy for sickle cell disease
  • RNA interference — discovered in C. elegans worms (Nobel Prize 2006), now the basis of approved drugs that silence disease-causing genes
  • Transposon engineering — "jumping genes," first discovered by Barbara McClintock in corn, now harnessed to insert therapeutic genes into human cells. The Sleeping Beauty transposon was reconstructed from ancient fish genomes for use in cancer immunotherapy
  • Ancestral reconstruction — scientists can resurrect ancient proteins to study how they evolved, and engineer more stable versions for drug development and industrial applications
  • Convergent evolution — bats and dolphins independently evolved echolocation through the same gene mutations, revealing which molecular designs are truly optimal and guiding protein engineering and drug design

Agriculture and environment

  • Crop improvement — wild relatives of wheat, rice, and tomato are crossed with cultivated varieties to introduce disease resistance and stress tolerance. Pangenome projects now sequence dozens of wild relatives, revealing genes lost during domestication that breeders can reintroduce
  • Conservation — the tuatara, sole survivor of its order, is prioritized because it represents a unique evolutionary lineage

Much of this began as basic curiosity — open-ended inquiry that produced breakthroughs no one anticipated.
